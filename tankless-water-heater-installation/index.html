A tankless water heater works on a simple idea: instead of keeping 40 or 50 gallons hot around the clock, it heats water only while a tap is open. Flow through the unit trips a sensor, a powerful burner or electric element fires, and the water is heated as it passes through a compact heat exchanger. The benefits are real: no standby heat loss, hot water that does not run out during back-to-back showers, a wall-mounted unit that frees up floor space, and a service life that often reaches twenty years with maintenance. For plenty of Green Bay, WI households, that makes tankless a smart upgrade.

Sizing is where tankless installs succeed or fail. We size by flow rate and temperature rise: add up the fixtures you realistically run at the same time in gallons per minute, then factor in how cold the incoming water gets in winter, since a unit that delivers five gallons per minute at a mild rise may deliver far less when the groundwater is cold. Then comes the infrastructure. A whole-home gas unit can draw several times the gas of a standard tank, which often means a larger gas line or a meter check. Venting must match the unit, and electric tankless models can need well over 100 amps, sometimes enough to require a panel upgrade.

Every tankless install from Green Bay Water Heater includes isolation service valves so future descaling takes minutes instead of hours, a clean gas connection with a sediment trap, correctly pitched and terminated venting, and a pressure relief valve piped to a safe location. If your water is hard, we talk through scale protection, since mineral buildup is what shortens tankless life most. We can add recirculation for faster hot water at distant bathrooms, set the temperature, handle permits where required, and walk you through maintenance before we leave. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Standby losses on a tank that sits idle for long stretches
  • Limited floor space in a closet, garage, or utility room
  • Renovation work that makes gas line and vent changes easier to fit in
  • Preferring a heater that can be repaired and maintained instead of replaced every decade
  • More people or more fixtures than your current heater was sized for
  • An aging tank that has started to rust, rumble, or leak
  • A far-off sink or bath where a point-of-use unit could help
  • A tank that cannot keep up with your household's morning routine

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Could a handy homeowner install tankless? Because the risks and the details stack up quickly. A burner that can draw nearly 199,000 BTU needs properly sized gas piping, and leaks or undersized lines are both dangerous and costly. Venting has to match the unit exactly, with the right material, slope, and termination clearances, or combustion gases can end up where they do not belong. Electric units can overwhelm an older electrical panel, and warranty terms often depend on a qualified installation. Permits and inspections are common requirements as well.

We take the guesswork out of going tankless. That starts with sizing for your busiest hour and coldest water, then confirming your gas, venting, and electrical can support the unit. We install with service valves for descaling, proper relief valve discharge, and venting built to the manual, then run the unit at full flow before we leave. If a tankless unit is not the best choice for your home, whether because of cost, gas supply, or usage patterns, we say so and help you compare other options honestly.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Keep the inlet screen clear so flow stays strong and the unit fires reliably
  • Check the vent cap outside for nests, debris, or buildup
  • Hard water shortens tankless life, so ask about scale protection
  • In freezing weather, keep the unit powered and insulate exposed piping
  • Pay attention to error codes; they point to issues that are cheaper to fix early
  • Descale the heat exchanger once a year, or more often with hard water
  • A setting near 120F limits scalding and reduces scale formation
